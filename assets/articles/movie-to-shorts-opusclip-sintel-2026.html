Movie to Shorts With OpusClip: Turn Sintel Into Shorts the Legal Creative Commons Way

By Qasim Kayani · Qasim Media

Instagram · Services & Pricing · WhatsApp

A long film can sit unwatched while its best moments never reach the people who would love them. A short clip is a different invitation: one scene, one idea, judged in seconds. That is why creators turn long videos into Shorts, and why OpusClip exists. It analyses a long source video, finds self-contained moments, and formats them as short vertical clips with captions and reframing. The idea is simple. The legal part is where most creators go wrong, and it comes first in this article.

The example on the thumbnail is not a random film. It is Sintel, the 2010 animated short from the Blender Foundation, shown in a YouTube search with the Creative Commons filter selected and labelled Creative Commons and Blender Foundation. Sintel matters because its licence allows reuse. Most films do not. If you paste a commercial film into a clipping tool, it may still produce clips, but producing clips is not permission to publish them. The licence is the permission, and Sintel is a source where it is real, provided you follow its attribution rules.

The Licence That Makes This Legal

Sintel is a Blender open movie, made with Blender, the free and open source 3D software supported by the Blender Foundation. Both the finished film and its production data were released under the Creative Commons Attribution licence. The Blender Foundation's sharing page for the Durian project, which produced Sintel, states that the results are licensed under Creative Commons Attribution 3.0, that you can freely reuse and distribute the content, including commercially, for as long as you provide proper attribution, and that the attribution depends on how you use it. If you redistribute, screen, or broadcast the movie itself, include the entire credits scroll. In all other cases, attribute the work as copyright Blender Foundation with the Durian project site or the Sintel site. Logos and trademarks are excluded. Those are the rules to follow, not a summary to paraphrase loosely.

The thumbnail shows the workflow in the right order: search first, filter second, tool third. On YouTube, the Creative Commons filter sits under the search filters, in the Features section. It limits results to videos marked with the Creative Commons Attribution licence, CC BY, the only Creative Commons option a creator can choose on YouTube. It lets others download, remix, and reuse the video, including commercially, as long as they credit the creator. Creative Commons is not public domain. The work is still under copyright; the licence is a standing permission with one firm condition, attribution. The film strip on the thumbnail shows adventure scenes, but only the Sintel panel is labelled Creative Commons. Do not read the strip as a licence for every scene. If a video carries the standard YouTube licence, the default position is that you do not have permission to clip and re-upload it. Check the filter, then the video's own licence line and description. If they disagree, believe the more restrictive one.

Using OpusClip, Then Checking Every Clip

OpusClip is an AI video repurposing tool. You give it a long source video, by upload or by link depending on your plan, and it segments the recording into candidate moments and formats them as short vertical clips, usually with 9:16 reframing, animated captions, and a ranking to help you choose. The thumbnail shows the same idea: a YouTube link pasted into OpusClip, a button labelled Generate 10 Shorts, and ten numbered vertical thumbnails. Treat that label as what the thumbnail shows, not a promise. Ten candidates are not ten finished Shorts. Some will start mid-sentence, some will end before a moment lands, and some will carry a caption error or a crop that cuts the action.

Use the tool as a first-cut editor, then watch every candidate in full. Does the clip make sense on its own, without the minutes before and after it? Are the captions accurate, including names and places? Does the vertical reframing keep the subject in frame throughout? Keep the clips that pass all three checks, and fix or discard the rest.

Attribution belongs in the Short description, where a viewer can find it: Sintel, the Blender Foundation, and the licence, with a link back to the original video, using the wording Qasim confirms. Do not suggest the Blender Foundation endorses your clips; the licence does not imply endorsement. If you changed the material, say so plainly. A clipped, captioned, reframed Short is a changed version. Add something of your own, too: a reason the moment was chosen, commentary on why it matters, or a series structure that turns scattered scenes into a guided tour. Do not present a Sintel clip as your own film. Before publishing, watch each Short once with sound off, to see whether the captions alone carry the moment, and once on a phone, to check crop and caption size.

Start small. Find Sintel through the Creative Commons filter, confirm the licence, and keep the link and attribution wording in a reusable note. Finish no more than three clips properly, publish them, and only then decide whether a larger batch is worth the review time. A small batch done properly teaches more than ten rushed clips.
