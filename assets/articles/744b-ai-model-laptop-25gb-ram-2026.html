744 Billion Parameter AI Model on a Laptop: What the 25 GB RAM Claim Really Means

By Qasim Kayani · Qasim Media

Instagram · Services & Pricing · WhatsApp

A 744 billion parameter AI model running on a laptop sounds impossible. For years the rule was simple: huge models need a data centre, or at least a powerful graphics card. The thumbnail for this article challenges that rule head on: "A 744 Billion Parameter AI Model Running on a Laptop", with "25 GB RAM", "370 GB Free Space", "No Graphics Card", and a laptop screen showing "Generating... 2%" beside a tortoise icon.

The tortoise is the honest part. Public reports say this can be done, but very slowly. This article explains what those reports actually describe, why the model can fit at all, and why fitting is not the same as being usable.

What Reports Describe

Search results from 1 November 2026 point to the same story from several independent write-ups. A project called Colibri, by a developer known as JustVugg, is reported to run GLM-5.2, a 744 billion parameter model from Z.ai, on a consumer machine with about 25 GB of RAM and no graphics card.

GLM-5.2 is described in those reports as a mixture-of-experts model. That phrase matters. Although the model has 744 billion parameters in total, only a fraction are active for any single token, reported as about 40 billion. Think of a huge library where each question needs only a few shelves. You still have to store the whole library, but you do not read it all at once.

Colibri is reported to keep the part used on every token, the dense core of about 9.9 GB after four-bit quantisation, in RAM, and to stream the remaining expert sections, about 370 GB, from a local solid-state drive as they are needed. The model is also reported to be written in pure C with no graphics card required, and to work offline once downloaded.

Why It Can Fit at All

Two ideas make this possible. The first is the mixture-of-experts design above: most of the model sits idle for most tokens. The second is quantisation, or aggressive weight compression. Shrinking the numbers that describe the model, in this case to four-bit precision, cuts its size without, reports say, destroying its accuracy. Together they move the bottleneck from memory to storage.

In plain terms, the drive becomes an extension of RAM. The computer keeps a small working set in memory and fetches expert sections from disk on demand, much as streaming a film fetches only the part being watched. That is why the reported storage need is so large, about 370 GB, and why the type of drive matters. A slow drive means slow fetching, and slow fetching means slow answers.

Why It Is So Slow

The thumbnail shows generation at 2%, with a tortoise beside the progress bar. Reports agree with that picture. One write-up says that on the developer's test machine, with a drive delivering about 1 GB per second, cold generation reached only about 0.05 to 0.1 tokens per second. At that speed, a short paragraph can take minutes, and a long answer can take far longer.

Other reported results are faster on stronger hardware. The same write-up lists about 1.06 tokens per second on an Apple M5 Max system with 128 GB of unified memory, in one configuration.

What It Is Not

This is not a practical replacement for a cloud chatbot on low-end hardware. Reports are explicit on that point: yes, a model of this size can technically run on a consumer machine without a graphics card, and no, that does not make it a usable everyday assistant there. If you need fast, interactive answers, a smaller local model or a cloud service will feel far quicker.

Privacy is the genuine upside reported for local runs: once downloaded, the model can run with no data leaving the device. That is not a trust fix on its own, as a local model can still give wrong answers.

Who This Suits, and What to Check First

This suits tinkerers, learners, and anyone who wants to understand how a frontier-size open-weight model behaves on their own hardware, and who can accept waiting. It does not suit anyone who needs quick answers, long documents in minutes, or a reliable daily assistant on a modest laptop.

Before trying anything like this, check the official project page, the exact model version, the licence, and the current hardware requirements, because projects like this change fast. One report already notes later updates adding more model families. Check that you have the free space, a fast drive, and enough RAM, and do not download hundreds of gigabytes on the strength of a thumbnail alone. If you run it, record your own machine, drive, RAM, and tokens per second, so the next claim can be checked against a real run.
