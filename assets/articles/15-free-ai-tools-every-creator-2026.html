15 Free AI Tools Every Creator Should Try in 2026

By Qasim Kayani · Qasim Media

AI content creator & video editor helping creators and brands grow on YouTube, Instagram and TikTok with AI video editing, thumbnail design and UGC ads.

Instagram · Services & Pricing · WhatsApp

Fifteen tools is a lot. Nobody needs all fifteen on day one, and nobody should try to learn them all in one sitting. The value of this list is different: each tool below does one job, and together the fifteen cover the whole job of a creator — making videos, making images, designing thumbnails, writing scripts, building presentations and websites, preparing for interviews, editing on a phone, and researching a topic properly before recording.

This list comes from a single video idea: fifteen free tools every creator should try in 2026. Each tool is named for the one job shown on screen. Use the list as a menu. Pick the job you are stuck on, try the tool named for that job, and judge it by the work it produces for you. If a tool does not help your work, leave it. Free only matters when the work gets done.

The fifteen tools

The simplest way to use this list is one job at a time. Start with the job that takes you the longest today. If writing takes longest, start with the content idea tools. If editing takes longest, start with the video tools. If thumbnails take longest, start with the design tool. Work through one tool properly — one real piece of content, start to finish — before adding the next one.

Keep your expectations honest. A free tool is a starting point, not a finished production team. The tools below are presented as free in the video this article follows. Free access, limits, and features change, so check what each tool currently offers before you build a regular workflow around it. No tool on this list is a substitute for checking your facts, your images, and your final video before you publish.

1. Qwen — Make Videos

What it does. Qwen is listed first for making videos. You describe the video you want, and the tool generates moving footage from that description. For a creator, the practical use is short supporting clips: an opening shot, a transition scene, or a visual for a line in a script that has no filmed footage.

How to use it. Use it for the shots you cannot film. Keep prompts short and specific — one subject, one action, one setting — and review every clip before it goes into an edit. Generated video still needs your edit, your captions, and your judgment about whether the clip actually fits the story.

2. Google Flow — Make Videos

What it does. Google Flow is listed second for making videos. Like Qwen, its job is turning a description into video. Having two video tools on the list is useful because no single generator handles every kind of shot well, and comparing two results for the same idea usually gives you a better clip than accepting the first one.

How to use it. Use it the same way: short clips, one idea at a time, reviewed before use. If one tool gives you a weak result for a particular shot, try the same idea in the other before changing the idea itself.

3. Grok — Make Images

What it does. Grok is listed for making images. You describe the image you need, and the tool generates it. For creators, the everyday use is thumbnail backgrounds, video cover images, and visuals for posts where a photograph is not available or not practical.

How to use it. Use it for backgrounds and supporting visuals, then finish the design yourself. A generated image is raw material. The thumbnail still needs clear text, a clear subject, and a layout that reads on a small phone screen.

4. ChatGPT — Make Images

What it does. ChatGPT is listed twice on this list, first for making images. You describe the image, and the tool generates it, and you can ask for changes in plain words: a different background, a different colour, a simpler composition. That back-and-forth is the useful part — you refine the image by talking about it, instead of starting over each time.

How to use it. Use it when you know roughly what you want but not exactly. Describe the result, look at it, then ask for one change at a time. Keep the final image simple. A thumbnail with one clear subject beats a busy image every time.

5. Canva — Thumbnails

What it does. Canva is listed for thumbnails. It is the design workspace where the pieces come together: your image, your words, and your layout. The thumbnail is the first thing a viewer sees, and Canva is where that first impression is built, with text, shapes, and ready layouts you adapt to your own content.

How to use it. Use it for every thumbnail, and keep the same rules every time: a few words at most, one clear subject, and text large enough to read on a phone. A thumbnail that needs explaining has already failed. Test it small before you publish.

6. Interviews by AI — Mock Interviews

What it does. Interviews by AI is listed for mock interviews. It runs practice interviews so you can rehearse answers before a real one. For creators and freelancers, the use is preparation: practising how you explain your work, your rates, and your process out loud, before a client or an employer asks.

How to use it. Use it as rehearsal, not as a script. Practise the questions you find hardest, listen to your own answers, and fix the places where you hesitate or over-explain. The goal is to sound clear and prepared, not rehearsed word for word.

7. Swishy AI — Templates

What it does. Swishy AI is listed for templates. Templates are starting points: a ready structure you adapt instead of building from a blank page. The time saved is the point — you change the words, the images, and the details, and the structure does the rest.

How to use it. Use templates to move faster, then make the result yours. Change the text, the images, and the layout until it matches your content. A template that still looks like everyone else's template has not been finished.

8. Gamma — Presentations

What it does. Gamma is listed for presentations. You give it a topic, and it builds presentation slides from that topic. For creators, the use is straightforward: a talk, a client pitch, a workshop deck, or a carousel that needs a clean slide structure without hours of manual formatting.

How to use it. Use it for the first draft of a deck, then edit every slide. Check the facts, shorten the text, and remove any slide that does not earn its place. A good presentation is short, visual, and easy to follow — no tool can decide that for you.

9. Canva — Presentations

What it does. Canva appears again on this list, this time for presentations. The same workspace that builds thumbnails also builds slide decks, which is useful when your thumbnail style and your presentation style need to match. One design workspace, one set of colours and fonts, used for both jobs.

How to use it. Use it when a presentation needs to look like your channel or your brand. Keep slides consistent — the same fonts, the same colours, the same spacing — and keep the words on each slide to a minimum. The slide supports what you say; it does not replace it.

10. Framer — Websites

What it does. Framer is listed for websites. It builds websites with a visual editor, so you can design and publish a site without writing code by hand. For a creator, the use is a portfolio, a landing page, or a simple site that shows your work and how to contact you.

How to use it. Use it for a site with one clear job: show your work and make contact easy. Keep the pages few, the text short, and the contact step obvious. A portfolio that takes ten clicks to reach is a portfolio nobody reaches.

11. Emergent Labs — Websites

What it does. Emergent Labs is listed second for websites. Like Framer, its job is helping you build a website without starting from code. Two website tools on the list gives you a choice: compare what each one makes from the same idea, and keep the result that looks and works better for your content.

How to use it. Use it the same way: one clear site, few pages, obvious contact step. Judge the result on a phone first, because that is where most visitors will see it.

12. Meta AI — Content Ideas

What it does. Meta AI is listed for content ideas. When you know your topic but not your next video, it helps you turn one topic into many ideas: different angles, different hooks, and different formats for the same subject. The value is in the list of options, not in any single suggestion.

How to use it. Use it to fill a content calendar, then choose with your own judgment. Pick the ideas that match what your audience actually asks about, and drop the rest. An idea list is only useful when most of it gets thrown away.

13. ChatGPT — Content Ideas

What it does. ChatGPT appears again on this list, second for content ideas. Like Meta AI, its job here is helping you find angles, hooks, and outlines for your next piece of content. The difference is the conversation: you can take one idea and keep asking for variations, simpler versions, or a full outline, in plain words.

How to use it. Use it to turn one idea into a plan: hook, three main points, and a closing line. Then write the content in your own words. The outline is the tool's job; the voice is yours.

14. Instagram Edits — Edit Video

What it does. Instagram Edits is listed for editing video on the phone. It is the editing app for creators who shoot and edit on mobile: trimming clips, adding captions, and preparing vertical video for Instagram and other short-form platforms, without moving to a computer.

How to use it. Use it for short videos you shoot on your phone, end to end. Edit, caption, and review on the same device your audience will watch on. If a caption is hard to read or a cut feels rushed on your phone, it will feel worse on theirs.

15. Perplexity — Deep Research

What it does. Perplexity is listed last, for deep research. Before you record, it helps you research a topic properly: what is known, what is new, and what sources say. For a creator, research is what separates a confident video from a guessed one, and this is the tool named for that job.

How to use it. Use it before you write the script, not after. Research first, note what you actually verified, and keep the sources you relied on. If you cannot verify a claim, leave it out. A shorter video you can stand behind beats a longer one you cannot.

Putting the fifteen together

Put together, the fifteen tools form one working day. Research the topic with Perplexity. Turn the research into ideas with Meta AI or ChatGPT. Write the plan. Make the video clips with Qwen or Google Flow. Make the images with Grok or ChatGPT. Build the thumbnail in Canva. Edit the video in Instagram Edits or your usual editor. Build the presentation in Gamma or Canva when the content needs slides. Put the finished work on a website built with Framer or Emergent Labs. Practise the pitch with Interviews by AI. Start from a template with Swishy AI whenever a blank page would slow you down.

That is the whole workflow, and it is deliberately ordinary. No single tool here does the work for you. Each one removes one slow step, and the steps add up. The creator who tries one tool, finishes one piece of content with it, and repeats that process will get more from this list than the creator who opens all fifteen and finishes nothing.

An honest note before you start

A final honest note. Every tool on this list is presented as free in the video this article follows, and every tool's free access can change. Before you depend on any of them, check what it currently offers, what limits apply, and whether it still does the job named here. If a tool has changed, replace it with another tool that does the same job — the workflow matters more than any single name on the list.

Start with one. Pick the job that slows you down most, try the tool named for it, and finish one piece of content. Then pick the next job. Fifteen tools, tried one at a time, will teach you more than fifteen tabs open at once.
